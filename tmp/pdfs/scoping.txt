Developing COLREG compliant Autonomous Surface Vessel Navigation 
using Behavioural Trees for busy water ways 
Summary  
Maritime autonomy is becoming increasingly prevalent in maritime environments. How to 
navigate in increasingly crowded waterways with variable traffic and rules, is a challenge of 
utmost importance for navigation systems. This dissertation builds on the ba ckground of this 
problem. Autonomous Surface Vessels (ASVs) must detect and avoid static and dynamic hazards 
and follow the rule set for the interaction of vessels at sea, the International Regulations for the 
Prevention of Collisions at Sea (COLREGs). 
revious work in the area has examined rule -based COLREGs compliance and several types of 
obstacle avoidance, but has a remaining issue. Reactive systems suffer in dynamic scenarios, 
and planning systems fall short in real -time requirements. This dissertati on builds on this 
problem. 
The framework proposed in this dissertation uses Behavior Trees as the primary decision-making 
system due to their clarity and suitability for maritime scenarios. Perception relies on LiDAR, to 
detect and track vessels around the ASV as conditions change. Motion planning is implemented 
through a combination of Artificial Potential Field (APF) methods for reactive responses to local 
threats, and Gaussian Process Motion Planning 2 (GPMP2) for smoother and globally planned 
paths. 
Testing will be performed on a simulated multi-vessel environment in Webots reflecting common 
COLREGs encounter types. Additionally, the project involves performing tests on a physical 
platform to observe real-world system performance.

Introduction 
Autonomous Surface Vessels (ASVs) have transitioned from theoretical concepts to real -world 
deployment in an increasing number of maritime application domains such as environmental 
monitoring, harbor surveillance, offshore inspection, and more recently car go transport. (Bae 
and Hong, 2023) Across these challenging environments, such as ports, harbors and congested 
coastal waterway. These man-made waterways present a dense traffic of vessels of varying sizes 
within constantly changing configurations of obstacles, which provides a unique environment for 
experienced human seafarers. (Hu et al., 2022) Adding static infrastructure and navigation 
boundaries, and considering requirements of compliance with the International Regulations for 
Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs), the challenge and lack of margin for error in autonomous 
navigation are significant. Traditionally, marine navigation has depended on pre -defined routes, 
waypoint following, or simple reactive avoidance methods. (Xu and Guedes Soares, 2023) While 
these approaches work well in environments where conditions change slowly and the chance of 
vessels interacting is low, they fail in scenarios where multiple vessels approach and depart from 
one another over time and space, where maneuvering is constrained, and where timely decisions 
must be made. (Burmeister and Constapel, 2021; Zhang et al., 2021) Navigation in these 
environments cannot be reduced to solving a geometry problem, but rather requires interpreting 
the behavior of surrounding vessels and predicting how it will evolve over time. (Li et al., 2024) 
An autonomous navigation system for the sea must not only sense and react to risk of collision in 
realtime, but do so in a way that other mariners can infer and anticipate. Safe motion at sea is 
partly a physics problem, partly a social problem: it requires navigation conventions that produce 
predictable, readable behavior. COLREGs encapsulate those conventions, and any truly 
autonomous navigation system operating in mixed traffic streams alongside human -crewed 
vessels will have to incorporate that regulato ry logic seamlessly with perception and planning. 
Doing the real -time obstacle tracking, rule -based reasoning, and then motion planning in 
between is not trivial. 
A number of papers have looked at solutions to parts of this problem. Velocity -based collision 
avoidance methods search velocity space for motion commands that are safe and dynamically 
feasible given predicted future interactions. (Cho et al., 2019) Their real-time responsiveness 
makes them particularly suitable for dynamic realtime deployment. (Cao et al., 2025) 
Probabilistic trajectory optimization techniques have also been developed to produce globally 
coherent smooth trajectories under uncertainty, taki ng into account the variations in sensor 
measurements, vessel motion and environmental conditions. These are useful for navigation in 
cluttered waterways with many possible interactions, when careful trade -offs between safety 
and efficiency must be made. B oth these kinds of approaches tend to be restricted to local 
planning and control, rather than the global high -level reasoning that true maritime navigation 
requires.

Behavior Trees (BTs) provide an attractive solution to this problem. Originaly designed as a 
modular decision architecture for robotics and game AI, BTs hierarchically organize complex 
behaviors as combinations of simpler tasks and conditions, which can be  composed, reused, 
and tweaked without affecting the broader system. (Colledanchise and Ögren, 2018; Iovino et al., 
2022) They also scale better than finite state machines as system complexity increases, because 
new behavioral logic can be added without overhauling the entire decision architecture. In some 
contexts, such as safety -critical systems, this transparency is more than just an engineering 
convenience. Understanding the logic behind a vessel maneuver is vital for debugging, 
verification, and ultimately, the creation of justified confidence in autonomous decision making. 
(Merino-Fidalgo et al., 2025; Alamoush and Ölçer, 2025) 
This work seeks to integrate the perception, planning, and decision-making capabilities of a route 
navigation system, using BT as the coordinator of perception and planning capabilities. 
Perception provides detection and tracking of nearby vessels, and sit uational awareness of the 
surrounding environment and other hazards. Planning interprets this awareness to generate safe 
and efficient motion trajectories. The behavior tree, on top of both, selects the appropriate 
navigation action to take in a given encounter situation and according to the rule for compliance 
with the COLREGs. 
Project aims and objectives. 
Aim: This project is aimed at developing and validating a behavior tree -based guidance and 
navigation framework for small autonomous surface vessels, to enable safe, resilient and 
COLREGs-compliant operation in settings with a high number of moving ships and obstacles. 
Objective 1: The creation of a modular behavior-tree-based architecture to serve as the top-level 
guidance and decision -making structure for autonomous surface vessels. The architecture 
should be flexible enough to describe a range of navigational tasks an d to encode rule -based 
behaviors in a formal, understandable manner. 
Objective 2: The design of behavior tree -based navigation behaviors that comply with COLREGs 
and allow the vessel to correctly determine right -of-way and execute appropriate collision -
avoidance manoeuvres for a variety of multi-vessel encounter scenarios. 
Objective 3: The integration of relevant Lidar data into the decision pipeline, to enable perception-
based, uncertainty-informed navigation in complex, dynamic marine environments. 
Objective 4: The development of a high -fidelity simulation environment in Webots, supporting 
multiple moving vessels, realistic traffic flows and port -scale layouts, to provide a realistic 
testbed for evaluating the proposed framework. 
Objective 5: Performance assessment of the proposed solution for different traffic densities, 
sensor availability and encounter configurations, to gain insight into the strengths and limitations 
of the described behavior tree -based approach and to inform i ts future development and 
application in autonomous vessel traffic management systems. 
Background and Literature review 
Autonomous navigation in maritime environment is an active research field, with many different 
approaches proposed for collision avoidance, path planning, and navigation in complex waters. 
Since maritime environments are dynamic, uncertain environments the navigation rules impose, 
no single method is capable of solving all aspects of the problem satisfactorily. (Burmeister and 

===== PAGE 4 =====

Constapel, 2021) Current research directions in the field includes reactive local planning, 
velocity-based collision avoidance, optimization -based trajectory generation, model -based 
predictive control, and sensor-driven perception. To perform well in highly dynamic, multi-vessel 
environments, each approach has specific strengths, but also their own weaknesses. (Han et al., 
2026) 
 
One of the most widespread approaches is the Artificial Potential Field (APF), where the motion 
of a vessel is directed by a virtual force field where the goal generates an attractive force, and the 
obstacles generate repulsive forces. The simplicity and low-cost of this method, together with the 
ability to deploy it in real-time, has made it an effective local obstacle avoidance and short-path 
modification technique for maritime vehicles. However, the characteristics of APF and its known 
drawbacks limit its application in maritime navigation. In particular, the method can be trapped 
in local minima when the attractive and repulsive forces balance out, or exhibit oscillations or 
other unstable behaviors close to obstacles or constrained movement areas. (Lyu et al., 2024) 
Since APF is mostly driven only by local geometric information, it is well known that it can 
generate suboptimal routes in cluttered environments. Efficient variants of the method have 
introduced effects such as relative velocity and directio n of motion of obstacles to allow 
navigation in dynamic settings using smoother avoidance paths. (Li et al., 2024) Nevertheless, 
APF-based methods remain reactive and local in their basic form, and their ability to encode high-
level rule-based navigational intent is limited. 
 
The Velocity Obstacle (VO) represents collision risk in velocity space, rather than in physical 
space. Instead of planning a trajectory around the obstacles, VO defines the set of vessel 
velocities which will result in a collision during a certain future t ime horizon. Safe navigation is 
achieved by choosing a velocity outside of this forbidden region (Yu and Roh, 2024). VO is 
therefore suitable for dynamic multi -vessel navigation, since it explicitly considers the relative 
motion between the own vessel and the surrounding targets. Extensions of the method 
considered constraints derived from the COLREGs, and hence it is a promising method for rule -
compliant navigation in busy waterway. (Thyri and Breivik, 2022) The success of VO depends on 
the accuracy of the  motion prediction and the available vessel sensor data. In case of noisy 
measurements or uncertain motion of the surrounding vessels, the collision region in velocity 
space might differ from the actual future encounter, which can result in over -conservative or 
insufficient avoidance. (Wu et al., 2026). In dense traffic, the available velocity space might 
become so limited that safe, efficient and rule -compliant navigation is impossible. (Cao et al., 
2025) 
 
Probabilistic trajectory optimization methods formulate navigation as an optimization over 
trajectories, providing a higher -level planning perspective. Gaussian Process Motion Planning 
(GPMP) has gained interest because the trajectory is represented as a c ontinuous-time 
stochastic process and can be optimized via probabilistic inference. In this framework, the 
uncertainty can be represented explicitly, the resulting trajectory can integrate the dynamics of 
the vessel and can plan over long horizons rather t han reacting to the immediate next threat. 
(Mustafa Mukadam et al., 2018) These are all desirable characteristics in a complex maritime 
environment, where the optimization needs to incorporate trade -offs between safety, efficiency 
and smoothness over time.  However, these advantages come at a cost: re -optimizing in a 
dynamic scenario with multiple obstacles can be computationally expensive. (Meng et al., 2022) 
Moreover, the cost functions, uncertainty models and non-linear constraints need to be specified 
correctly; otherwise, the trajectory produced may be optimal in a mathematical sense but 
undesirable in practice. In order to be used in real -time in a safety -critical maritime system, a 
number of compromises is often needed.

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is the other major research line that views the navigation problem 
as a constrained optimization over a finite prediction horizon. The controller predicts the future 
vessel states with a dynamic model and chooses control inpu ts which minimize a cost function 
while respecting safety and dynamic constraints on the optimization decision variables. (Zhang 
et al., 2022) The unified formulation enables vessel dynamics, actuator limits, obstacle 
avoidance and navigation goals to all be considered simultaneously and in maritime applications 
has been demonstrated to incorporate additional COLREGs related requirements in the 
predictive decision making. But its drawback lies in the need to model for accurate system 
representation and tuni ng of parameters. The maritime dynamics are generally nonlinear and 
affected by environmental disturbances such as wind, waves and currents that need to be 
considered. A non -accurate model will cause prediction errors that will result in poor control 
performance. (Zhang et al., 2025) Poor tuning will result in increased conservatism or poor trade-
off between safety and efficiency and the computational cost may increase rapidly when there 
are many constraints and moving obstacles. Overall, it is powerful but needs to be implemented 
effectively to realize its full potential for real-time maritime navigation. 
 
From a perception perspective, LiDAR has taken a more prominent role, especially for 
applications in ports and harbors, where a high spatial resolution and close -range accuracy are 
essential. LiDAR sensors can provide high-density geometric information about the surroundings, 
which is thus well suited for tasks such as obstacle detection, environmental mapping, and 
short-range situational awareness.(Villa, Aaltonen and Koskinen, 2020) This data can even be 
used in dynamic environments for detecting and trac king moving obstacle with a processing 
pipeline consisting of filtering, clustering, data association, and multi -target tracking in order to 
transform the raw point cloud data into meaningful representations on which the planning and 
control modules can op erate.(Xie, Nanlal and Liu, 2024) Its performance, however, is also 
affected by unfavorable weather conditions such as rain, fog, and sea spray, and by reflective 
water and vessel motion. It also offers limited semantic insight compared with vision -based 
sensors. Therefore, in practice, LiDAR data are frequently combined with camera, radar, GNSS, 
and IMU sensor data to provide a more reliable source of situational awareness through the 
fusion of sensor data.(von Brandis, Menges and Rasheed, 2024) 
 
Taken together, this body of work shows a pattern that runs across all the papers. While there is 
plenty of work in each module, particularly perception and local planning, there is still no total 
architecture that unifies the sensing, rule interpretation,  behavior selection and trajectory 
generation in a coherent and explainable way. Some papers have a robust perception pipeline, 
but not much decision logic. Some paper advanced planners, but they are based on unrealistic 
assumptions on the detection of obstacles, or on knowledge of the scenario. This is an important 
issue, because safe maritime navigation, in the definitive sense of the word, is not a meager low-
level control problem, but a complex high -level decision-making problem. A navigation system 
must be able to compute collision threats, recognize maneuver types, interpret COLREGs 
responsibilities, and select a proper planning response. This must be done in an explainable way, 
that can be verified. In the more advanced papers, all these functions are achieved successfully, 
but not all in a single driver. What is needed in the field is an integrated architecture that links the 
perception module with the planner in an explainable decision -making layer, and with the 
controller. The added version should i mprove not just functional performance, but also 
interpretability, maintainability, and safety assurance. 
Methodology / Planned work  
The proposed framework has four components: perception, decision-making, path planning and 
simulation. Instead of separating the components into independent modules, the suggested 

===== PAGE 6 =====

architecture ensures that the output of each layer feeds the next one, allowing the system to act 
in a coordinated manner on its environment. 
The goal of the perception module is to detect and track the objects in the surroundings based on 
the raw sensor data. The process starts with a preprocessing step and the extraction of features, 
which feed the clustering algorithm that aggregates the sensor returns and transforms them into 
individual objects. Then a tracking algorithm is executed, which estimates the motion of the 
objects over a period of time, and predicts their future initial condition. These predictions are 
used by the path planning and decision layer, to create the situational picture on which navigation 
decisions are based. Therefore, the accuracy and speed of the perception module are vital for the 
system performance. 
The decision -making module is implemented as a copy of a Behavior Tree. The navigation 
behaviors are structured hierarchically, and specific behaviors can be easily added or replaced. 
The decision layer encodes the navigational rules of COLREGs on encounte r and right -of-way 
responsibilities as decision nodes of the tree. This structure allows the navigation decision to be 
easily traced and justifiable by the system, which is mandatory for a safety -critical system. At 
each time step, the tree is traversed fo llowing the current situation, providing a decision on the 
mental model of the intended behavior. 
Path planning is performed via a hybrid mechanism between a reactive planner and an 
optimization-based approach. Characteristic of the former is immediate responsiveness to local 
collision threats, which allows for local motion adjustments as the environment changes without 
computationally expensive look -ahead. The second mechanism plans over a longer horizon, 
generating smooth, dynamically -consistent trajectories that trade between safety, efficiency, 
and vessel dynamics. Incorporating both mechanisms allo ws for responsiveness to the 
environment for the former while still allowing for route and encounter-level planning for the latter. 
The simulation environment will be built in Webots and will be the primary testbed for the system. 
The environment will include realistic port-scale layouts, various numbers of moving vessels, and 
realistic traffic volumes to evaluate the performance of the system under various conditions, 
such as varying traffic densities, encounter geometries, and sensor availabilities, in or der to 
establish the capabilities, failure modes, and boundaries of the proposed behavior -tree-based 
approach before attempting the implementation of the system on a physical vessel. 
Environmental and societal impact [~200 words] 
The development of autonomous navigation systems for maritime applications carries 
implications that extend well beyond technical performance. At a fundamental level, systems 
that reduce reliance on human judgement in high -pressure navigational situations have the 
potential to lower the incidence of collision-related accidents, many of which stem from fatigue, 
inattention, or miscommunication between vessels. In busy waterways, where the 
consequences of such accidents can be severe and wide-reaching, this represents a meaningful 
contribution to maritime safety. 
From an environmental standpoint, more efficient navigation directly translates to reduced fuel 
consumption and lower emissions, supporting broader efforts toward sustainable maritime 
operations. There is also a less direct but equally important benefit: r obust collision avoidance 
reduces the likelihood of incidents that cause environmental damage, such as hull breaches 
leading to oil spills or cargo loss that disrupts marine ecosystems. 

===== PAGE 7 =====

These benefits must, however, be weighed against genuine costs. The deployment of sensor -
heavy autonomous systems carries its own energy burden, and the lifecycle of advanced 
computational hardware raises questions about electronic waste that should not be  dismissed. 
Perhaps more significantly, the gradual automation of navigational roles raises legitimate 
concerns about employment in an industry where human expertise has historically been central. 
These are not abstract concerns, and responsible developmen t of autonomous maritime 
systems requires that they be taken seriously rather than treated as secondary to technical 
objectives. 
Addressing these tensions is partly a matter of design choices. Optimising algorithms to reduce 
computational load, selecting energy-efficient hardware, and building systems that are intended 
to assist rather than wholesale replace human operators are all practical steps that can reduce 
the negative footprint of autonomous navigation technology. The goal is not automation for its 
own sake, but the development of systems that make maritime operations safer, cleaner, and 
more reliable in ways that account for their broader impact.
