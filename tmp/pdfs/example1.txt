Abstract 
This paper presents an experimental study of the efficiency and flow pattern of different thickness 
foils with prescribed pitching and free heave motion. The frequency of pitching oscillations has been 
fixed at 𝑓∗ = 0.15 and the pitching amplitude varied between 𝜃0 = 50 𝑜 & 130𝑜. Numerical findings 
from previous studies have suggested that increasing the foil thickness  delays leading edge vortex 
shedding, increasing the efficiency of flapping foil syst ems. This investigation has e xperimentally 
confirmed this finding. The maximum efficiency for the thick foils was found to be 33.8%, significantly 
higher than the medium or thin foils tested. For all foils tested a vortex formed at the leading  edge, 
causing the flow to reattach to the foil su rface. Once the angle of attack reached a certain angle this 
vortex shed and the foil lift dropped. The angle at which vortex shedding occurred increased with the 
foil thickness. For the medium thickness  foils the flow reattachment increased the period of high 
power production, increasing the efficiency. For the thick foils the vortex showed little impact on the 
period of power production. But, the maximum power was significantly higher, resulting in a 
significantly high er efficiency. Although correct timing  of the leading edge vortex  shedding can 
increase the efficiency for medium thickness foils, the thick foils have a much higher power across the 
oscillation period. In addition, the thick foil continues to increase in maximum efficiency at a ngles of 
attack whe re the medium foils begin to drop off. This results i n a significantly higher power and 
efficiency for the thick foils.

1 Introduction 
The global demand for electricity is expected to grow between 60-90% by 2040 (International Energy 
Agency, 2018). This means that there is now, more than ever, a need for alternative sources of clean 
energy. Tidal turbines can produce a predictable and sustainable method for clean energy generation 
and, as a result, tidal stream energy converters have become a focus for renewable energy research.  
Flapping foils have several advantages over the more conventional turbine-based generation devices. 
They have a lower environmental impact without the large centrifugal stresses associated with 
rotating blades. In addition, the swept area for a flapping foil device is wide, with a narrow extent of 
vertical motion, allowing for installation in shallow water (Xiao & Zhu, 2014). The concept was first 
proposed in 1981 (McKinney & DeLaurier, 1981) for a windmill which utilized a harmonically oscillating 
wing to extract wind energy. Previous studies have investigated the power extraction efficiency (𝜂), 
due to the impact of the reduced frequency (𝑓∗) and the pitching amplitude (𝜃𝑜) of NACA0012 and 
NACA0015 foils (Kinsey & Dumas, 2008; Simpson 2009; Zhu 2011; Kinsey & Dumas; 2011 & Xu, Xu & 
Dai 2017). These studies found that high efficiencies (up to 𝜂=45%) occurred at high pitching 
amplitudes (𝜃𝑜≈75𝑜) and a reduced frequency of 𝑓∗≈0.15,  at which point dynamic stall occurs. 
Recent work has investigated designs for flapping foil systems using reduced actuation. All the studies 
previously mentioned use fully prescribed motion, where both the heave and the pitch motions are 
fully controlled. This allows for parameters, such as the amplitudes, frequencies and phase difference 
to be prescribed. At the other end of the spectrum are fully passive systems, where there is no 
prescribed motion and the foil is free to respond both in pitch and heave. This is achieved due to the 
self-sustained fluttering motions of the foil, such as those proposed by (Peng & Zhu, 2009; Veilleux & 
Dumas, 2017; Boudreau et al., 2018 & Jiang et al., 2019). Fully passive systems are very appealing as 
they minimise the mechanical complexity of the system, reducing inefficiencies, expense and risk of 
failure. The middle ground between these two designs are the semi-actuated systems, which can be 
free to oscillate in either heave (Zhu & Peng, 2009; Zhu, Haase & Wu, 2009; Deng et al., 2015; Griffith 
et al., 2016; & Zhan et al., 2017) or pitch (Boudreau, Gunther & Dumas, 2018). These systems still 
allow for control over some of the experimental parameters, without the complexity of fully 
prescribed motion. Flapping foil systems only require a small amount of energy to drive the pitching 
mechanism and the heave motion predominantly accounts for the energy extraction. Therefore, free 
heave systems are the logical choice, allowing for control, whilst minimising complexity and requiring 
only a relatively small input energy.

The impact of the leading edge vortex (LEV) on the efficiency of flapping foils has been analysed 
extensively. It is widely believed that the timing of the LEV shedding is crucial to the synchronisation 
between the vertical force and heaving velocity, maximizing the power extraction efficiency (Kinsey & 
Dumas, 2008 & Zhu & Peng, 2009). The LEV can have a significant impact on both the lift of a foil and 
the stall point. The presence of the LEV causes the flow, that would usually be separated, to reattach 
to the foil, allowing the foil to achieve large angles of attack whilst maintaining attached flow over the 
trailing edge, in a phenomenon termed ‘delayed stall’ (Sane, 2003). A large angle of attack allows the 
foil to produce very high lift coefficients, due to the larger angles of attack imparting a greater 
downward momentum to the fluid, resulting in a significant enhancement of the lift force. For thin 
blunt aerofoils the air moves more sharply around the leading edge of the foil, generating greater 
vorticity and a larger LEV (Polhamus, 1971), see Figure 1. As the angle of attack of the foil continues 
to increase to higher angles of attack the LEV grows until flow reattachment is no longer possible. At 
this point the trailing edge flow is no longer attached and the Kutta condition breaks down, causing a 
significant drop in lift.

The effect of foil thickness on the LEV has been numerically analysed in two previous studies of foil 
shape. Wang et al., (2016) found that thick foils (NACA0025 & NACA0027) form a smaller LEV 
producing a higher efficiency, because for the thin foil (NACA0006) the vortex sheds earlier, reducing 
the foil lift force. Sun et al., (2017) also found that a thick aerofoil has a higher energy extraction 
efficiency. The large thickness foil (NACA0025) increased the work done by the heave force and 
produced high efficiencies (up to 𝜂=54%). They found this is due to the smaller LEV’s forming on 
the thick foils and the retarding of the LEV shedding, Figure 1. Both studies show that thick foils can 
achieve a larger angle of attack before a reduction in power, directly contributing to the increase in 
efficiency, Figure 2. 
The aim of this study is to experimentally investigate the impact of foil thickness on the efficiency of 
power production for a free heave flapping foil system. To do this a flapping foil devise will be designed 
and built to measure the potential efficiency of power extraction, which will be measured for a range 
of foil thicknesses and pitch amplitudes. The experiment will endeavour to confirm or deny the 
computational results found by Sun et al., (2017) and Wang et al., (2016), that foils thicker than the 
traditional NACA0012 & NACA0015 foils result in higher potential power production efficiencies. 
Analysis of the LEV will be performed using flow imagery to investigate the correlation between foil 
thickness, LEV shedding and the resulting impact on the efficiency.

2.2 Experimental Set-up  
The experimental rig used consists of two foils mounted either side of a central heave post, as shown 
in Figure 6. The foil setup and connections have been designed and built specifically for this study, 
details of the design and construction are given in Appendix 1. The experiments were carried out in 
the University of Southampton flow tank, details are given in Appendix 4. The foils are free to move in 
heave and have prescribed pitch motion. The pitch is driven with a chain drive system with the motor 
at the top of the heave post, out of the water. The pitch was controlled using a 3 Amp NEMA23 stepper 
motor controlled with an Arduino. The stepper motor was programmed to follow a sine wave profile 
using a pulse width modulation (PWM) method, for more detail see Appendix 3.

2.3 Instrumentation  
Two FX1901 compression load cells, with a maximum load rating of 45.4 kg, were used to measure 
the force acting upon the body. The signal from the load cells were passed through an amplifier to get 
a value readable by the Arduino, see Appendix 1. The load cells were mounted underwater between 
the central post and the foil pitch bearing. The load cells required calibration to transfer from the 
amplified voltage signal to the force, detail of this process is given in Appendix 5. As these are 
compression load cells, a rig needed to be devised to mount the load cells, so they were only pulled 
in compression, details on this rig are given in Appendix 1. The heave position was also measured to

coloured dye into the water upstream of the foils and visually recording the flow over the foils,

2.4 Data Processing 
The typical turbine run performed five oscillations at each value of 𝜃0 with a period 𝑇 of 6.67 seconds 
per oscillation, resulting in a total run time of 33.33 seconds. Each run was repeated five times for 
each foil giving a total of 25 oscillations per angle per foil. These oscillations were averaged by using a 
least squares fit method with a degree of fitting up to 𝑡15. This method of averaging the data from the 
oscillations ensured that the detail of the data was maintained and had good conformity. The data 
had a small average percentage difference of 2.8% from the recorded values and the least squares 
average, due to the high repeatability of the experimental measurements.

3 Results and Discussion 
3.1 Efficiency  
In order to assess the change in efficiency of different foil thicknesses all the parameters have been 
fixed apart from the pitch amplitude 𝜃0 and the foil profile. Figure 7 shows the effect of the pitch 
amplitude 𝜃0 on the efficiency η for different thicknesses of foils. The maximum efficiency is 
significantly higher for the thick foils (NACA0030) compared to the thin  (NACA0010) or medium foils 
(NACA0015 &NACA0020). For the medium and thick foils, the efficiency at low angles of attack (𝜃0<
100) is roughly equal. But the efficiency of the thick foils continues to increase at higher values of 𝜃0, 
where the power drops for the thin or medium foils, at 𝜃0≈100. This continued efficiency increase 
lead to a  maximum efficiency of η=33.8%±6.7 for the NACA0030 foil at the highest angle of attack 
tested 𝜃0=130𝑜. The efficiency of the thin foils are significantly lower than the other foils at low 
values of 𝜃0 and are still lower than the medium thickness foils even after 𝜃0≈100 where the 
medium thickness foils efficiency drops.

3.3 Heave  
The efficiency has large errors, see Figure 7, due primarily to the heave being assumed to follow a 
sinusoidal pattern, equation 10. To validate this assumption the heave position was measured, Figure 
9 shows a plot of the measured heave position against the modelled position. From these values the 
position does not follow the sine wave profile and indeed the heave distance also varies between 
oscillations. This is not surprising because firstly, the power profile of the foils does not follow a 
sinusoidal pattern, especially at high values of 𝜃0. In addition, the designed system is heavy relative to 
the foil lift, resulting in a large force being required to overcome the inertia of the system. The heave 
post system design also has friction associated with the bearing which needs to be overcome before 
motion can occur. These factors led to the heave motion being erratic and unreliable. To try and 
quantify this error the average percentage difference was calculated between the theoretical and the 
measured results, with an average difference of 16.8%. The assumption of the phase difference 𝜓=
90𝑜 between the pitch and heave response is also a difficult assumption to verify due to the erratic 
nature of the heave motion. The total average percentage error is 19.7%, the other errors come from 
the force data (2.9%) and the calibration data.

3.4 Flow Visualization 
Analysis was performed on the flow pattern around the different foil thicknesses (Figure 10). All the 
foil thicknesses show the same flow mechanisms, which is that as the flow moves over the foil with 
an increased angle of attack the flow over the leading edge of the foil separates and begins to stall. 
The detachment leads to a vortex in the separation zone, causing the LEV, see Figure 10. This flow 
separation then reattaches before reaching the trailing edge of the foil. As a result, the pressure 
differential between the upper and lower surfaces of the foil increases due to the flow reattachment. 
This increase in the pressure differential leads to an increase in the lift force of the foil and therefore 
an increase in the power. As the value of 𝜃0 continues to increase the LEV sheds into an unsteady 
wake and there is a significant decrease in the power. The timing at which this LEV shedding occurs is 
directly impacted by the thickness of the foils.  
For the thin foils there is a large LEV formed at low values of 𝜃0, which sheds early in the oscillation 
period, at relatively low angles of attack. This means that for much of the oscillation period the flow 
has detached from the foil surface, coinciding with a lower efficiency for the thin foils.  


===== PAGE 20 =====

20 
 
The medium foils show a large LEV, which remains attached for longer than for the thin foils before 
shedding. This flow reattachment can be seen in the flattening of the peak power for the medium foils 
at  𝜃0=90𝑜 & 100𝑜 (Figure 8). The flow reattachment causes an increase in the power later in the 
oscillation period and increases the average power across the oscillation period. This is where the 
highest efficiencies for the medium foils can be found, shown in Figure 7. Beyond this value of 𝜃0 the 
LEV can be seen to be shed from the surface and to no longer have flow reattachment, this 
corresponds with the decrease in lift, see Figure 11.  
The thick foils power profile does not show the flattening off of the medium foils , instead following a 
sinusoidal profile. Logically this would be because of no LEV shedding for the thick foils, as found by 
Sun et al., (2017), however this is not the case. The thick foils can clearly be seen to form and shed a 
LEV. However, the vortex is expectedly smaller than for the thin foils. The shedding of the LEV 
happened later in the oscillation period. Therefore, the flow is attached for longer and no flatter peak 
is seen, as the shedding happens after the maximum power has been reached. With the later timing 
of the LEV shedding, the efficiency is significantly higher for the thick foils. This matches the numerical 
findings of Wang et al., (2016) who found that the highest efficiencies are found when there is a 
relatively thin blade LEV which dissipates gradually. Additionally, Kinsey & Dumas (2014) found that 
for high Reynolds numbers the optimal efficiency cases do not depend upon well timed LEV shedding. 
Instead maximum power extraction is found between mostly attached boundary layer s and the 
beginning of LEV shedding occurrences.  
The thick foils reach a higher maximum efficiency at a larger angle of attack than the medium or thin 
foils, matching the findings shown in Sun et al., 2017, see Figure 2. Although, the experimentally found 
value of 𝜃0 for which maximum efficiency occurs is higher than the current numerical literature. Sun 
et al., (2017) found that the maximum efficiency for the NACA0030 foil occurred at 𝜃0≈100o which 
is similar to the finding for the NACA0020 foil in this report, but lower than the found value for the 
NACA0030 foil. The reason for this higher angle of attack is probably due to the foil motion.  The erratic 
nature of the heave motion had a significant impact upon the effective angle of attack. This is 
important because the effective angle of attack in a cycle has the main influence on the peak forces 
generated and on the occurrence dynamic-stall with LEV shedding. In addition, the motion of the foil 
seems to act to physically cast off the LEV and without the accuracy of this motion the LEV may stay 
attached for longer than if the heave was more pronounced and followed a sinusoidal pattern. The 
maximum efficiency found is lower than that found by Sun et al., (2017), although this is to be 
expected, due to this study being experimental rather than numerical, plus the pitching contribution 

===== PAGE 21 =====

21 
 
to power not being considered for this report. The efficiency is also lower due to this experiment using 
free rather than prescribed heave. 
These results do raise some interesting questions. Notably where does this effect end and what is the 
optimal foil thickness? Taking the foil thickness to its logical conclusion would result in cylindrical foils. 
Griffith et al. (2016) found that cylinders have a smaller heaving amplitude and velocity, significantly 
reducing the energy extraction potential. They also found that the lift coefficient was higher for the 
thicker ellipses and cylinders. This suggests that there is an optimal power between a high heave 
velocity and a high lift coefficient. There are an extremely large number of parameters which can have 
a significant impact upon the potential efficiency of the system. These results do not consider input 
energy which would be higher for larger values of 𝜃0. Although, as mentioned previously, the amount 
of power required to actuate the pitch motion is significantly lower than the heave power output.  
This finding suggests that the use of fully passive systems could be problematic, whilst maximizing the 
system efficiency. As found by Sun et al., (2017) the retarded vortex shedding for the thick foils means 
that there is a reduced pitching moment acting upon the foil. While this reduction in moment has little 
impact upon the total power production and efficiency it could have a significant impact upon a fully 
passive system, where the pitching moment is crucial for sustained oscillations. The results of this 
investigation show that to reach the highest efficiencies thick foils are required, making implementing 
a fully passive system more difficult.

4 Conclusions 
This paper focuses on the efficiency of different thickness foils (NACA0010-NACA0030) over a range 
of maximum pitch amplitudes (𝜃0=50 𝑡𝑜 130𝑜), with a fixed frequency (𝑓∗=0.15). This was done 
by designing and building an experimental testing setup with driven pitch and free oscillations in 
heave. This is the first time for flapping foils that different thickness foils have been investigated 
experimentally, and this paper aims to experimentally investigate the validity of the numerical 
findings. In addition, this paper has analysed the flow characteristics, to determine the effect of foil 
thickness on the flow mechanisms and the impact of these mechanisms on the efficiency.  
• The efficiency for thick foils (NACA0030) was found to be significantly higher than 
medium or thin foils. This maximum efficiency occurs at a very high pitching amplitude 
where 𝜃0=130𝑜. The erratic nature of the heave motion almost certainly had a large 
impact upon the results. Therefore, the exact values should be treated with caution, but 
despite this the general trend is clear.  
 
• This paper significantly advances the understanding of flapping foil technology in that it 
validates the numerical findings of Sun et al., (2017) & Wang et al., (2016).  
 
• The timing of the vortex shedding can improve the efficiency for medium thickness foils, 
but this  is not as important as ensuring that flow is attached to the foil at higher angles 
of attack. The medium foils formed flatter peak power curves, due to the reattachment 
of the LEV at values of pitching amplitude 𝜃0≈90𝑜 𝑡𝑜 100𝑜. This reattachment caused 
a higher average power over the oscillation period and therefore a higher efficiency. 
Above this value of 𝜃0 the flow no longer reattached, and the efficiency dropped. For the 
thick foils the LEV shedding happened later in the oscillation period than for the thin or 
medium foils. This meant that the thick foils did not show the flat power peaks of the 
medium foils, however the power produced was significant ly higher, resulting in a 
significantly higher efficiency. LEV shedding did occur for the thick foils, but it was past 
the peak power for the cycle.  
 
• The use of thick foils is paramount for reaching the highest efficiencies possible for 
flapping foil systems. These findings, along with the numerical studies also show the 
potential of flapping foil systems to reach high efficiencies approaching the Betz limit of 
59%. The use of thick foils also has an impact on fully passive systems as they have a 

===== PAGE 24 =====

24 
 
reduced pitching moment associated with the LEV shedding. This pitching moment is 
crucial for self-sustained oscillations.  
 
• Flapping foil systems have a huge number of parameters which effect their potential 
power production and more testing is certainly required. Investigating thicker foils and 
the optimum foil thickness would be extremely useful to determine the limit of this effect. 
In addition, the impact of applying a generator to the system would provide an interesting 
investigation. Taking the power out of the system would reduce the vertical heave motion 
and could impact the optimum pitching amplitude and foil thickness.

Appendix 1 - Experimental Design  
The experimental setup used for these experiments presents a new design for a flapping foil test 
apparatus. The design is based around a central heave post, similar to that more traditionally used for 
towing tank experimentation, such as seakeeping. Figure 12 shows a comparison schematic of the 
new system used compared to the more traditiona l setup. The new setup solves some design 
challenges but also creates some of its own. There are two main reasons for the design change. Firstly, 
the centre of lift of the two foils is in line with the heaving direction, reducing the bending moment 
on the bearing mechanism. This bending moment causes additional friction in the bearing mechanism.  
In addition, the new design can operate with free heave as the heave motor can easily be detached 
from the system. There is also the added advantage that this system could more easily be incorporated 
into existing towing tank heave post designs.  
This design also poses several challenges. Firstly, the system needs neutral buoyancy, otherwise the 
system would either sink to the bottom or float to the surface. In addition, the central heave post is a 
large bluff body in the flow which could cause a significant amount of drag, resulting in loading on the 
heave bearing, adding friction. Both issues were solved by the addition of a fairing to the central 
column, increasing the buoyancy of the system. The central fairing also ensured a smooth flow around 
the heave post, reducing drag and possible vortex induced vibrations around the central post. The 
system had a natural restoring spring force due to the change in buoyancy as the heave post lifted out 
and sank into the tank, changing the immersed volume but not the system weight. This restoring force 
had little impact on the overall result due to the relatively small diameter of the heave post and the 
already erratic heave motion.  
Another challenge posed was due to the heave bearing. The bearing used was a Bosch Rexroth Linear 
Ball Bearing, but due to the vertical distance between the foil and the bearing it was unknown if the 
system would have enough rigidity using a single bearing. As a result, an initial design prototype was 
made and tested in the flow tank. From the results of this testing it was clearly apparent that a second 
bearing was required as well as a sturdier bearing housing. The number of supports used for the setup 
was also increased to improve the stiffness.  
Figure 13 shows the writing diagram for the motors, with the motor drivers and the load cells with the 
load cell amplifiers. The motion was controlled with an Arduino for simplicity and to allow for both 
the motors and load cells to be controlled by a single device. The motors were powered by a 12V 
supply from the mains to ensure a consistent power supply.

Appendix 2 – Preliminary Testing  
There were two rounds of preliminary testing on the experimental setup. The first round of testing 
was performed to assess the structural requirements of the system. This testing involved actuating 
the system in pitch and in heave by hand with no flow and analysing the stiffness of system. There 
were two main questions that this testing aimed to answer. Firstly, the initial design had a single linear 
bearing for the central post and it was unclear if this single point of contact would provide the required 
rigidity. The second question that needed to be answered was whether the central column would have 
a low enough friction to allow for non-actuated heave motion. From this testing it was clear that the 
single bearing did not provide enough support for the heave post. In addition to this, the system 
required more support, so aluminium extrusion supports were added above the bearing housing as 
well as below. The bearings showed that they could transfer the force into motion without significant 
friction. The testing also revealed that bigger motors were required as the pitching motor would slip 
when required to lift the weight of the motor. The bigger motors had no such problems.  
The second round of testing was performed with the full system and a flow velocity. The initial aim 
was to have two rounds of testing, one which utilised driven heave for a more direct comparison to 
the literature, and one with free heave oscillations, to show the potential of a more realistic 
commercial device. The heave was designed to be actuated by using a rack and pinion design, with 
the heave motors fixed on top of the heaver post next to the pitching motors. The motors used had 
the required speed and torque to lift the assembly. However, after extensive attempts the heaving 
actuation had to be abandoned. This was because the rack and pinion design had difficulty transferring 
the torque and would result in the post slipping and falling. There were a couple of potential reasons 
for this. Firstly, the mounting of three motors on top of the steel post meant that the system had a 
high weight which needed to be lifted by the motors. In retrospect, mounting the heave motors on 
the top of the heave post was a design oversight. Also, the gearing system needed perfect alignment 
with the rack and pinion which proved challenging within the time restraints. There were only a couple 
of days in which testing was possible and correcting these issues was not possible within the 
timeframe. Ultimately the free heave scenario was more important for this report and therefore the 
decision was taken not to analyse the driven heave.

Appendix 3 - Pitch Motion Definition 
The stepper motors were programmed to follow a sine wave profile using a pulse width modulation 
(PWM) method, to produce a digital signal. This was programmed manually in the Arduino software 
(see Appendix 8) by programming a sine wave with the desired profile, rounding this number to the 
nearest whole number and then misusing the previous rounded value. If this number was one then 
the motor would take a step, if the number was zero the motor would remain stationary. It was 
important to control the frequency of the profile to ensure a balance between motor speed and 
accuracy of the profile. Both the pitch and heave motors were controlled in the same method, with 
the heave motors running in opposite directions.

Appendix 5 - Load Cell Calibration 
Due to the nature of the load cells used, calibration is required. This is because the load cells output a 
voltage, which was amplified by an electrical amplifier and produced an amplified voltage relative to 
the force applied. To transfer from this amplified voltage to a force the following equation was used 
𝐹=𝐶∗𝑉𝐴 (13) 
where 𝐹 is the force, 𝐶 is the calibration constant, and 𝑉𝐴 is the amplified voltage from the load cell. 
To find the calibration coefficient known forces were applied to the foil. These were used to apply 
both a tensile and a compressive force to the load cell setup, the output was then recorded. In total 
25 different forces were applied to the apparatus both in tension and compression to produce the 
calibration coefficient. The calibration setup was as similar to the final setup as possible. For the 
calibration the load cell assembly was removed, and the calibration weights were placed on top of the 
load cell, to measure the compression. To measure the tension, the weights were hung underneath 
the load cell assembly, see Figure 18. The average percentage difference for the tension and 
compression load cells calibration coefficients were 0.0432% and 0.0291% respectively. The 
calibration showed good conformity with all the results being close to the trendline. The data was 
acquired using an Arduino coded with a refresh rate of 0.125 seconds. A total of 50 readings were 
taken from the Arduino and the average used to find the amplified voltage.
